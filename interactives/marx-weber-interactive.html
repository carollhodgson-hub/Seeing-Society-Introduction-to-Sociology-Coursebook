How Did They Define Social Class?

Karl Marx

Class is rooted in production

For Marx, the most important class division concerns a person's relationship to the means of production—the land, factories, tools, technology, and capital used to produce wealth.

Do you own productive property, or must you sell your labor to survive?

Max Weber

Class is rooted in market position

For Weber, class concerns a person's economic opportunities in the marketplace, including property, income, education, credentials, and valuable skills.

What resources and opportunities shape your life chances?

In one sentence: Marx emphasizes ownership and conflict between major economic classes; Weber offers a more multidimensional and finely graded picture of stratification.

A surgeon may earn a very high income but still work for a hospital rather than own the institution. A factory owner may earn through ownership. Marx focuses on their different relationships to productive property. Weber also examines income, specialized credentials, occupational prestige, and influence.

Part One

Karl Marx: Conflict, Production, and Power

Marx argued that economic organization shapes institutions, ideas, and everyday life. Under capitalism, those who own the means of production have structural power because they control jobs, investment, production, and much of society's wealth.

💰 Bourgeoisie

The capitalist class that owns or controls the means of production. Its members receive wealth through ownership and profit. In Marx's model, this class can influence working conditions, wages, politics, and dominant ideas.

🛠️ Proletariat

The working class, whose members do not own enough productive property to live from it and therefore must sell their labor for wages. Marx argued that employers seek profit partly by paying workers less than the full value their labor creates.

🧱 Lumpenproletariat

People pushed outside stable wage labor and organized production. Marx used the term for marginalized people without a consistent class position or connection to organized workers. The term is historically loaded and can be stigmatizing, so sociologists should use it as Marx's analytical category—not as a label for people today.

🧠 Class Consciousness & False Consciousness

Class consciousness occurs when workers recognize their shared class interests and organize collectively. False consciousness describes beliefs that obscure exploitation or lead people to accept arrangements that work against their class interests. The latter term is commonly associated with Marxist theory, though Marx did not develop it as a formal concept in the exact way later theorists did.

A key distinction: Communism in Marx's end-stage sense is not simply a government owning everything. A powerful one-party state is not the stateless, classless society Marx envisioned.

No modern nation is widely regarded by scholars as having reached Marx's fully classless and stateless communism. Countries commonly called communist—including the Soviet Union, China, Cuba, Vietnam, Laos, and North Korea—developed strong states governed by communist parties. Many officially treated socialism as a transitional stage on the path toward communism.

Small voluntary communities, communes, and some collective settlements have practiced extensive sharing or common ownership. Examples often discussed include early Israeli kibbutzim and intentional communes. Their limited size, voluntary membership, strong shared identity, and operation within larger market societies make them different from running an entire modern nation.

  • People and groups tend to pursue their own interests and compete for scarce resources.
  • Those placed in control of resources may use that position to protect and expand their own power.
  • Centralized control during a revolution can create a new political elite rather than eliminate hierarchy.
  • Large societies face difficult problems of coordination, information, incentives, accountability, and resource allocation.
  • Scarcity, disagreement, cultural differences, and competition do not disappear when ownership changes.

From the conflict-theory perspective, removing private ownership does not automatically remove competition or unequal power. A new group may gain control and become the dominant group.

These words represent the conflict-theory perspective; they are not a quotation from Karl Marx. If people and groups pursue their own interests, compete over scarce resources, and attempt to protect power once they obtain it, a completely classless society is unlikely to remain classless. Even when an old ruling class is removed, a new group of officials or leaders may gain control of resources and become a new power elite.

Part Two

Max Weber: Class, Status, Party, and Life Chances

Weber agreed that economics matters, but he argued that inequality cannot be explained through ownership alone. Power is multidimensional.

Class

People who share similar economic interests and market situations. Property matters, but so do education, credentials, occupation, and skills.

Status

Social prestige, honor, or respect. A person may have high status without high income—or wealth without acceptance by a prestigious group.

Party

Organizations that pursue shared goals and influence decisions. Political parties are examples, but professional associations, unions, and other organized groups can also exercise power.

Life Chances

Weber used life chances to describe the opportunities people have to obtain valued resources and experiences—such as education, housing, health, safety, and desirable work. A person's market position affects what is realistically possible, not merely what the person wants.

Types of Legitimate Authority

👑 Traditional

Obedience rests on long-established custom: “This is how authority has always been passed down.” Example: a hereditary monarch.

✨ Charismatic

Obedience rests on devotion to a leader believed to possess exceptional qualities. Example: a transformational movement leader.

📜 Rational-Legal

Obedience rests on impersonal rules and legally defined offices. Example: a judge whose authority comes from law, not personality.

Power is the ability to carry out one's will even against resistance. Authority is power regarded as legitimate. A robber may exercise power through threat, but that does not make the robber's command legitimate authority.

Bureaucracy & Rationalization

Weber saw modern societies becoming increasingly organized around calculation, efficiency, predictability, formal rules, specialized roles, records, and technical expertise. Bureaucracies can coordinate large organizations fairly and efficiently, yet they may also become rigid, impersonal, and difficult to escape—an “iron cage” of rationalized systems.

The Protestant Ethic

Weber examined how religious ideas—especially disciplined work, thrift, and a sense of calling in some forms of Protestantism—helped create a cultural climate compatible with the growth of modern capitalism. He did not claim that Protestantism was capitalism's sole cause.

🏁 Sociological Takeaway

Marx helps us see how ownership, labor, and class conflict organize inequality. Weber helps us see that economic position interacts with prestige, organizations, legitimacy, and bureaucracy. Together, they ask us to look beyond individual choices and examine the systems that distribute power and life chances.
