🔎 Opening Question: Why Do People Break Rules?

Biologists, psychologists, and sociologists all study deviance, but they look in different places. This chapter focuses on the sociological question: What is happening around the person?

🧬 Sociobiology

Looks for explanations inside the body, including genetics, body type, or inherited traits.

Older theories sometimes claimed body type, XYY chromosomes, or intelligence explained criminality. Sociology is cautious about these claims because they often ignore social conditions.

🧠 Psychology

Looks inside the individual mind, personality, coping patterns, trauma, or mental health.

Psychological explanations may focus on personality disorders, emotional distress, or individual coping. These can matter, but they still do not explain why deviance varies across groups, places, and time periods.

🌍 Sociology

Looks outside the individual at socialization, groups, inequality, power, labels, opportunity, and culture.

Sociologists ask how families, schools, peer groups, neighborhoods, social class, subcultures, and institutions shape behavior.

⚠️ Deviance vs. Crime

⚠️ Deviance

Behavior that violates social norms and causes a social reaction.

Example: loudly singing in a quiet library.

🚔 Crime

A violation of norms that have been written into law.

Example: robbery, assault, theft, or fraud.

Key idea: Not all deviance is criminal, and not all crimes are treated with the same level of social disapproval.

🧠 Thomas Szasz, Medicalization, and Mental Health

Medicalization of deviance means behavior once seen as sinful, strange, or criminal may be redefined as a medical problem requiring treatment.

👨‍⚕️ Szasz's Concern

Thomas Szasz argued that some behaviors labeled as mental illness may actually reflect social problems, stressful life experiences, coping behaviors, or behaviors society finds unacceptable. His argument has some merit when it reminds us not to ignore the social world around the person.

Sociological question: If social conditions change and symptoms improve, how much of the problem was created or intensified by the environment?

⚖️ A Balanced Sociological View

Sociology does not deny biology, psychology, or medical treatment. Instead, sociology adds another layer: relationships, stress, media, inequality, institutions, stigma, and coping behaviors can shape well-being.

📱 The Doom Scrolling Connection

Many students recognize this pattern: news alerts all day, scrolling crisis stories before bed, online conflict, and constant exposure to frightening information. Social science research has linked heavy exposure to distressing media and doom scrolling with worse mood, stress, anxiety, rumination, and sleep problems.

Changing coping behavior can change the environment: turning off news alerts, limiting doom scrolling, checking news at planned times, taking screen breaks, connecting with others, and improving sleep routines may reduce stress.

👥 Differential Association Theory

Edwin Sutherland argued that people learn deviance or conformity through the groups they associate with. Groups can teach skills, values, attitudes, and justifications.

Close relationships + repeated exposure + learned values = increased likelihood of similar behavior.
People can learn how to deviate and learn to value deviance.

🎭 Techniques of Neutralization

Sykes and Matza explained that people may reject negative labels by justifying behavior. Click each technique.

🚫 Denial of Responsibility

“It was not really my fault.” The person claims outside forces caused the behavior.

💥 Denial of Injury

“No one was really hurt.” The person minimizes the harm.

😡 Denial of a Victim

“They deserved it.” The person claims the target was not a true victim.

👨‍👩‍👧 Appeal to Higher Loyalties

“I did it for my family/friends/group.” Loyalty to one group is used to justify violating wider rules.

👉 Condemnation of the Condemners

“The people judging me are worse.” Attention shifts to the supposed hypocrisy of critics.

🔒 Control Theory and Social Bonds

Control theory begins with a different assumption: people may be tempted toward deviance, but controls and social bonds restrain behavior.

🧠 Inner Controls

Morality, integrity, fear of punishment, desire to be good, and self-control.

👥 Outer Controls

Family, friends, schools, police, jobs, and community expectations.

Hirschi's Social Bonds

❤️ Attachment

Strong connections to others encourage conformity.

🎓 Commitment

Investment in future goals discourages risky behavior.

⚽ Involvement

Time spent in legitimate activities leaves less room for deviance.

⚖️ Belief

Respect for conventional values and authority discourages deviance.

🌆 Illegitimate Opportunity Structures

Cloward and Ohlin argued that some communities have organized opportunities for crime woven into daily life, especially when legitimate opportunities fail.

🏫 Legitimate Opportunity

Strong schools, stable jobs, mentoring, safe neighborhoods, transportation, and legal pathways to success.

🚧 Illegitimate Opportunity

Drug markets, theft networks, gambling, prostitution, burglary, gangs, or “hustles” that appear to offer success.

⚔️ Conflict Theory and Crime

Conflict theorists focus on power: who makes laws, whose behavior is punished, and whose harm is overlooked.

🏛️ Who Writes the Rules?

Powerful groups often shape law and enforcement.

🚔 Who Gets Policed?

Law enforcement may focus more heavily on working-class and marginalized communities.

💼 Who Gets Protected?

Corporate or elite harm may receive less attention unless highly visible.

Conflict theory does not say street crime is harmless. It asks why some harms are punished more aggressively than others.

🚔 Types of Crime Gallery

🔪 Street / Conventional Crime

Crimes commonly associated with public fear, such as robbery, assault, burglary, theft, or drug offenses.

💼 White-Collar Crime

Crimes committed by people of respectable or high social status in the course of their occupations, such as fraud, embezzlement, wage theft, or unsafe working conditions.

❤️‍🩹 Hate Crime

A crime motivated by bias against a status or identity, such as race, religion, sexual orientation, political belief, subculture, occupation, or another stereotyped category.

🏆 Final Theory Detective Challenge

Choose the theory that best fits each scenario.

Scenario 1: A student is repeatedly called “troublemaker” and eventually stops trying to prove people wrong.

Best fit: Labeling theory. The label shapes self-concept and others' expectations.

Scenario 2: A person wants wealth and status but has blocked access to education and stable employment, so they turn to fraud.

Best fit: Strain theory. Cultural goals remain, but legitimate means are blocked.

Scenario 3: A teenager learns from friends how to shoplift and begins to see it as normal.

Best fit: Differential association. Deviance is learned through close relationships.

Scenario 4: A corporation harms workers through unsafe conditions, but the public focuses more on street crime.

Best fit: Conflict theory and white-collar crime.
