Model slide 2 · Introduction

Problem: decisions without public trust

My Ideal Society addresses declining trust in public decisions. The sociological imagination connects a resident’s feeling of powerlessness—a personal trouble—to the public issue of institutions that make decisions without meaningful participation (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026). A transparent civic portal would show proposals, evidence, public comments, and final votes. Because reality is shaped through repeated interaction, this system treats trust as a social construction, not a personality trait. The portal also changes social structure by giving residents regular access to decision makers (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026). Digital access alone could exclude some residents, so libraries and neighborhood assemblies would provide devices, interpretation, and face-to-face participation.

Purposeful visual: A flow diagram from resident concern → public deliberation → decision → explanation.

Model slide 3 · Section One

A culture of transparent responsibility

The society’s central values are dignity, transparency, and civic responsibility. An open-circle symbol appears on public records to mean that residents may inspect and question a decision. A key norm requires officials to explain how evidence and public input shaped a policy; recognition for clear explanations is a positive sanction (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026). Symbolic interactionism explains how repeated use gives the circle shared meaning. Conflict theory adds an essential warning: transparency can become performance if powerful groups still control what information is released (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026). Independent auditors and resident review panels therefore check omissions. The system protects trust, but it must also protect personal privacy when openness could expose vulnerable people.

Purposeful visual: The open-circle symbol beside three labeled values.

Model slide 4 · Section Two

Learning participation across the life course

Families are the first agents of socialization, teaching consent, care, digital safety, and how to ask institutions questions (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026). Schools reinforce these lessons through age-appropriate councils where children practice Mead’s role-taking by considering classmates’ viewpoints. Instead of an unspoken hidden curriculum of passive obedience, students learn respectful disagreement and evidence-based revision. Media-literacy mentors help families examine algorithms, privacy, manipulation, and online boundaries. Adults entering new civic roles receive anticipatory socialization before serving on public panels (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026). Guidance must not become surveillance, so children gain privacy and decision-making freedom as their skills develop. Protection means building trustworthy relationships and clear help pathways, not blaming young people for adult misconduct.

Purposeful visual: Concentric circles labeled family, school, peers, media, work, and community.

Model slide 5 · Section Three

Repair before exclusion

In this society, deviance means violating a shared norm, while crime means violating law; the categories overlap but are not identical (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026). Most first-time, nonviolent violations enter a Repair–Accountability–Belonging program. Restorative justice asks who was harmed, what repair is possible, and how recurrence can be prevented. This approach limits the labeling that can turn one act into a master status. Social control still includes proportionate formal sanctions when safety requires them, but it also uses positive sanctions for restitution and changed behavior. Conflict theory guides annual reviews of who is stopped, reported, punished, or excluded. The trade-off is that restoration can pressure harmed people to participate, so participation remains voluntary and independent advocates protect their choices.

Purposeful visual: A balanced pathway showing safety, accountability, repair, and reintegration.

Section Four · Interdependent institutions

A “Government for the People” council coordinates family, education, health, economy, and government. Use institution, manifest function, latent function, and interdependence; show how a childcare policy changes work, health, and school participation. Include a safeguard against concentrated power (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026).

Section Five · Equity with measurement

Audit access to income, safety, education, health, and voice using stratification, privilege, structural inequality, and intersectionality. Explain why equal rules can produce unequal outcomes and how affected communities share authority (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026).

Section Six · Humane adaptation

A resident assembly evaluates new technologies for access, environmental cost, labor effects, and cultural lag. Apply social change, innovation, social movement, and institutional reform. Include a pause-and-review process when harms emerge (Hodgson & Norwood, 2026).

Conclusion · What the design taught us

Return to public trust. Explain how culture, socialization, institutions, inequality, and change interact. Name one unresolved tension—perhaps transparency versus privacy—and show how sociological thinking changed the original solution.
