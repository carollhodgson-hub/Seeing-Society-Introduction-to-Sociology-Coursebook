October 14, 1895

Eleanor’s diary

The trains come more often now. Their whistles interrupt supper, church meetings, and sleep, yet Father says the sound means our town matters. New families arrive looking for factory work. Some neighbors call them strangers even after they have lived here for months.

From my window, the factory seems to promise two futures at once. Its lamps glow after dark and its wages give families money they did not earn from a farm. Still, children leave the gate with adults, and everyone walks as if the clock itself were chasing them.

People speak of progress as though it were a single road and everyone travels it together. I wonder who chose the road. I wonder why some ride the train while others lay the track.

Mother says a young woman should not ask so many questions in company. Perhaps she is right about what company expects. I am less certain that expectation and truth are the same thing.

If powerful people repeat a belief often enough, it begins to sound like common sense. I hope progress remembers the people expected to live through it.

Sociologist’s note
This diary is a fictional learning narrative grounded in social conditions of the late nineteenth century. It lets us connect biography, historical change, technology, gender norms, work, and power. Eleanor’s observations are a starting point, not evidence by themselves. Sociology asks how we could investigate them.
